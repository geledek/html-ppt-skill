# Singapore Standard English

A **locale pass**, not a style pass. Run it *after* `no-ai-slop`, which removes AI
patterns. This removes the American startup register that survives it, and which
models default to because their training skews that way.

Singapore Standard English is British-based, more formal than US business
English, and notably low on idiom. It is the English of MAS circulars, IMDA
frameworks and the Straits Times, not of a San Francisco product blog.

## What this is not

**Do not write Singlish.** No lah, leh, lor, can-can, "got or not". A formal
document written in Singlish reads as mockery, not localisation.

**Do not add local colour.** No hawker centres, MRT, kopi, or Merlion unless the
subject is actually those things. Localising register is not localising content.

**Do not over-correct into stiffness.** Formal is not bureaucratic. Short, direct
sentences are correct in every register.

## Spelling and forms

British throughout: organisation, recognise, analyse, licence (noun) / license
(verb), practice (noun) / practise (verb), programme (except computer program),
centre, defence, behaviour, judgement, enrol, fulfil, travelled, modelling.

Dates: 12 September 2026, never September 12, 2026. Never 9/12/26.

Currency: **S$** for Singapore dollars. Always qualify other dollars — "1.76
billion Australian dollars", never a bare "dollars", because in Singapore an
unqualified dollar is S$. Spell plurals when spoken: "15 million euros".

Collective nouns take a singular verb in formal SG usage: "The Authority has
issued", "the committee is".

## Americanisms to cut

| Cut | Use |
|---|---|
| reach out, circle back, touch base | contact, write to, ask |
| deep dive | examine, review |
| double down | insist, persist, commit further |
| move the needle | change the outcome |
| table stakes | the minimum |
| low-hanging fruit | the easy part, the obvious first step |
| out of the gate, off the bat | at the start |
| ballpark | approximate |
| gotten | got |
| ahead of (meaning before) | before |
| oftentimes | often |
| a couple things | a couple of things |
| different than | different from |
| write someone | write to someone |
| go-to | usual, preferred |
| across the board | in every case |
| at the end of the day | (cut it) |
| game changer, no-brainer | (state what it does) |

Also cut sports and military metaphor, which travels badly: home run, slam dunk,
curveball, moving the goalposts, in the trenches, bandwidth (of people),
firefighting, war room.

## Tics that survive a slop pass

These are not Americanisms and not slop, so both earlier passes miss them. They
are the drafting habits that make a document read as machine-written.

**"lands" / "doesn't land"** for succeeds or convinces. "The point lands." Cut it
and say what happens: "The point is clear", "readers will accept it".

**"real"** as an intensifier. "a real problem", "the real question", "genuinely
real". Almost always deletable. If it is doing work, name the contrast instead.

**"with you"** and similar loose prepositional attachments. "protects you with a
regulator", "that sits with me". Name the relationship: "protects you *in a
regulator's assessment*".

**"the thing is" / "here's the thing"** — cut, state the point.

**Stacked appositive dashes** as a rhythm crutch, which also cannot be heard when
read aloud.

**"worth knowing" / "worth flagging"** as a preamble. Say the thing.

## Register

- Prefer the passive where SG official writing does: "Firms are expected to…"
  is more natural than "We expect firms to…" in a compliance context. This is the
  one place where the usual active-voice rule yields.
- Address the reader as "you" sparingly in formal material; "an organisation"
  or "a firm" is often better.
- No rhetorical questions to the reader.
- No exclamation marks.
- Acronyms used by the audience need no gloss: MAS, IMDA, PDPC, PDPA, FI, SMF.
  Gloss anything outside that: ECOA, BEAD, FTT.

## If the text will be spoken

Sentences of 15–20 words. One clause of subordination at most. No parenthetical
asides — they have no audible equivalent. Read every sentence aloud; if you run
out of breath or lose the subject, split it.

## Output

Return the edited draft and a short **What changed** section grouped by rule
(spelling, Americanisms, tics, register), so the author can push back on a
category rather than on individual words.
