THREE QUESTIONS

Three questions for any AI proposal.

Accountability

Who is accountable when this is wrong, and what is their name?

Contestability

How does a person affected by this contest what it decided?

Disclosure

What have we told customers this does?

Few executives have time to assess every AI proposal properly. Most have time for three questions. Who is accountable when this is wrong, and what is their name? How does a person affected by this contest what it decided? What have we told customers this does?

Each comes from a case you just saw. Accountability: FEAT Principle 8 makes firms accountable for externally sourced models as well as their own. A third of AI use cases in UK financial services arrive through third parties. Contestability: the Dutch, Deliveroo and Foodinho findings all turned on whether a person could argue with a decision. Disclosure comes from Air Canada, and from the first AI-washing settlements brought by the SEC in 2024. Two advisers paid 400,000 United States dollars between them for claiming to use AI that they were not using. One caution on the third. A New York regulator reviewed roughly 400,000 Apple Card applications. It found no discrimination by Goldman Sachs or Apple, and both could explain every complainant's decision when asked. The public criticism continued anyway. Being able to explain a decision will protect a firm in a regulator's assessment. On its own, it will not protect its reputation.

A firm can obtain satisfactory answers to all three and still approve a system with no evidence that it works. A fourth question closes that gap. What evidence would justify approving this, restricting it, or stopping it? Four things make up that evidence. It has been tested in the setting where it will run, not on the vendor's benchmark. A person can intervene, and has the authority to overrule it. Monitoring would show if it had drifted. And a stop or rollback exists that somebody has actually tried.

Systems should not allow high stakes or irreversible actions to take place without human review.

IMDA · Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI

Singapore's agentic AI framework states the hard line. Systems should not allow high stakes or irreversible actions to take place without human review. It does not prohibit autonomy. It prohibits autonomy where the action cannot be undone.

QUIZ

Approve, restrict, or stop?

An AI tool flags insurance claims for manual review. It has a named accountable owner, a published appeals process, and accurate marketing copy.

It has been tested on the vendor's benchmark dataset. There is no monitoring in production and no rollback plan.

A
Approve. Accountability, contestability and disclosure are all in place.

Those three are necessary and not sufficient. None of them tells you whether it works in your setting.

B
Restrict. A limited pilot with monitoring and a rollback, then reassess.

The gap is evidence, not governance paperwork. A bounded pilot produces what a vendor benchmark cannot.

C
Stop. It cannot be deployed without production monitoring.

Defensible, and disproportionate here. The action is a flag for human review, which is neither high-stakes nor irreversible. Reserve stopping for decisions that are.
